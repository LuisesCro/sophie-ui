## Section 3 — The Book of Mormon & Scripture

### 21. What is the Book of Mormon?

The Book of Mormon is the founding scripture of the Latter-day Saint faith, a roughly 500-page volume that Latter-day Saints revere alongside the Bible as the word of God. Its subtitle, added in modern editions, states its central claim plainly: "Another Testament of Jesus Christ."

The book presents itself as an ancient record. According to its own account, it was written by prophets and historians living in the Americas between roughly 600 BC and AD 400, then engraved on golden plates, compiled by a prophet-historian named Mormon, and buried by his son Moroni. Latter-day Saints believe Joseph Smith recovered those plates in the 1820s and translated them "by the gift and power of God," publishing the result in 1830. That is why the book bears Mormon's name, and why members are sometimes called "Mormons" — though the church prefers members be identified by the full name of the faith.

For believers, the Book of Mormon is not a supplement or a curiosity but the "keystone" of their religion, a word Joseph Smith himself used. The logic is straightforward: if the book is genuinely an ancient record translated by divine power, then Joseph Smith was a true prophet and the church he founded is Christ's restored church. If it is not, the whole structure loses its foundation. This is why so much attention — devotional and skeptical alike — focuses on this single volume.

The heart of the book, for members, is its witness of Jesus Christ. Its climax describes the resurrected Christ descending from heaven to visit the ancient Americas, teaching, healing, and blessing the people there. Latter-day Saints read the book devotionally, quote it in sermons, and encourage newcomers to read it and pray about it. Whatever a reader concludes about its origins, the Book of Mormon is impossible to understand the faith without — it shapes the community's identity, its missionary work, and its sense of itself as a peculiar and covenant people.

### 22. Is the Book of Mormon a replacement for the Bible?

No. This is one of the most common misunderstandings about the faith, and the answer matters. The Book of Mormon is presented as an addition to the Bible, not a substitute for it. Latter-day Saints use both, and read them side by side.

The church's eighth Article of Faith states, "We believe the Bible to be the word of God as far as it is translated correctly; we also believe the Book of Mormon to be the word of God." Members study the Old and New Testaments in their Sunday curriculum on a rotating schedule, quote the Bible constantly, and hold it as scripture. The King James Version is the church's official English Bible. Far from discarding it, Latter-day Saints see their additional scriptures as confirming and clarifying it.

The intended relationship is one of mutual witness. Latter-day Saints point to a verse in Ezekiel about two "sticks" — one for Judah and one for Joseph — becoming one in God's hand, which they read as a prophecy of the Bible and the Book of Mormon joining together. In their view, the two books testify of the same Jesus Christ from two different ancient peoples on two different continents, each corroborating the other.

That phrase "as far as it is translated correctly" does signal a real difference from many other Christians. Latter-day Saints believe some truths were lost or altered during the long history of copying and translating the biblical text, and they see their additional scriptures as restoring clarity on points they feel the Bible left incomplete. This is part of why some other Christian denominations do not regard Latter-day Saints as sharing their view of the Bible's sufficiency. But the disagreement is about interpretation and completeness, not about rejection. If you visit a Latter-day Saint meeting, you will hear the Bible preached — alongside, not instead of, the Book of Mormon.

### 23. Where did the Book of Mormon come from?

The answer depends heavily on whom you ask, and this is one of the sharpest dividing lines in any honest account of the faith. Here are both accounts, fairly stated.

The believers' account begins with Moroni. According to Joseph Smith, the same Moroni who buried the golden plates around AD 400 appeared to him as an angel beginning in 1823, when Joseph was a teenager, and directed him to the plates buried in a hillside near his family's New York farm. Joseph said he obtained them in 1827 and translated their ancient engravings into English "by the gift and power of God," dictating the text to scribes. The plates, he said, were then taken back by the angel. The finished translation was published in 1830. For Latter-day Saints, the book is exactly what it claims to be: a real ancient record, delivered by miraculous means.

The critical account sees a 19th-century composition. In this reading, Joseph Smith — whether through deliberate authorship, sincere religious imagination, or some combination — produced the book himself out of the ideas, language, and questions circulating in his time and place. Skeptics point to the King James English woven throughout, to theological debates that were live in 1820s America appearing in the text, and to the absence of the plates for any independent examination.

What both sides tend to agree on is the striking raw fact at the center of the story: a young man with little formal schooling dictated a long, intricate, internally consistent book in a remarkably short span — a matter of weeks of concentrated work. Believers see this as evidence of divine help no farm boy could have managed alone. Critics see a naturally gifted storyteller and the genuine creativity of an unusual mind. The dictation is documented; the interpretation is where faith and skepticism part ways. The book itself invites readers to decide by a spiritual, not merely historical, test.

### 24. What is the Book of Mormon actually about?

At its core, the Book of Mormon tells the thousand-year story of a family and its descendants, and it is framed from beginning to end as a testimony that Jesus is the Christ. If you open it expecting a novel, you will find instead something closer to a compiled sacred history, full of sermons, wars, migrations, and prophecies.

The narrative opens around 600 BC in Jerusalem, just before the city's fall to Babylon. A prophet named Lehi, warned by God, leads his family out of the city, and they eventually build a ship and cross the ocean to a "promised land" in the Americas. There the family divides. The followers of Lehi's son Nephi become the Nephites; the followers of his son Laman become the Lamanites. Much of the book traces the long, often violent relationship between these two peoples across the centuries.

The dramatic and spiritual climax comes near the middle-end of the book. After Christ's crucifixion in the Old World — marked in the Americas by storms and darkness — the resurrected Jesus descends from heaven to visit the people there. He teaches them, echoing the Sermon on the Mount, heals their sick, blesses their children, and establishes his church among them. For Latter-day Saints this is the emotional heart of the entire volume: a second witness, on another continent, of the same risen Lord.

The book then follows the long decline that follows. Over generations, the people fall into pride and conflict, and the story ends in catastrophe, with the Nephite civilization destroyed in a final war. The prophet-historian Mormon abridges the whole record; his son Moroni finishes it, buries it, and — in the book's closing pages — invites future readers directly: read these things, and ask God if they are true. That invitation, known to members as "Moroni's promise," is the lens through which believers say the book is meant to be received.

### 25. Who were the Nephites and the Lamanites?

The Nephites and Lamanites are the two principal peoples of the Book of Mormon, and their long rivalry supplies much of its narrative drama. Understanding them helps make sense of the whole book — and also of one of its most sensitive interpretive questions.

Both groups descend from the single family of Lehi that, according to the book, sailed from Jerusalem around 600 BC. The Nephites take their name from Nephi, a faithful younger son; the Lamanites from Laman, an older brother portrayed as rebellious. In the book's telling, the Nephites are generally (though not always) the record-keeping, God-following people, while the Lamanites are frequently — though again not always — hostile. The book is careful to complicate this: at several points the Lamanites are more righteous than the Nephites, and the labels blur over time into political and religious loyalties as much as bloodlines.

Their relationship swings between war and uneasy peace across a thousand years. Some of the book's most quoted passages come from moments when the divide is bridged — mass conversions, missionary journeys between the peoples, and periods after Christ's visit when the distinction disappears entirely and the people live in harmony for generations. The final chapters describe that unity collapsing again, ending in the war that destroys the Nephites and leaves the Lamanites as survivors.

Here the modern sensitivity arises. For much of the church's history, Latter-day Saints commonly taught that Native Americans and Pacific Islanders were literal descendants of the Lamanites. This shaped missionary work and identity for many members. In recent decades, and especially in light of genetic research, the church has revised how it frames this. The introduction to the Book of Mormon once called the Lamanites "the principal ancestors of the American Indians"; it now reads "among the ancestors." Many members today understand the Lamanites as one group among many, or read the whole account through a limited-geography lens rather than a claim about two entire continents. It is a live example of a faith community working through the relationship between its scripture and modern knowledge.

### 26. Is there archaeological or scientific evidence for the Book of Mormon?

This is where the case for and against the book is fought most directly, and an honest answer has to present both sides squarely, because reasonable people weigh the same facts very differently.

The critical case is substantial. Unlike biblical archaeology, which can point to excavated cities, inscriptions, and named kings, no site, artifact, or inscription has been confirmed as belonging to the Nephites or Lamanites, and no non-Latter-day-Saint archaeologist recognizes the peoples the book describes. Critics also point to apparent anachronisms — the book mentions horses, steel, wheels, chariots, and Old World crops and animals that mainstream scholarship says were absent from the pre-Columbian Americas. And they cite genetics: DNA studies consistently show that Native American populations descend overwhelmingly from Asian ancestors who crossed from Siberia, not from a Near Eastern family arriving around 600 BC. Taken together, critics argue, the evidence points to a book composed in the 19th century rather than translated from an ancient one.

Believing scholars respond on several fronts. Many now favor a "limited geography" model, arguing the events took place in a relatively small region — perhaps parts of Mesoamerica — among far larger existing populations, so a small founding lineage's DNA could easily be swamped and undetectable today. They suggest some anachronistic words may be translations of unfamiliar things (a "horse" naming some other animal, for instance) and note that absence of evidence in a poorly excavated region is not proof of absence. They also point to features they find striking, such as the book's complex internal geography and Hebraic literary patterns.

Underlying the whole debate is a deeper question about what kind of book this is. The church's fundamental response is that scripture is not finally answerable to archaeology — that the Book of Mormon asks to be tested by prayer and spiritual witness, not by the spade or the laboratory. Critics counter that a book making concrete historical claims should leave historical traces. Where you land depends a great deal on which kind of evidence you consider decisive.

### 27. How did Joseph Smith say he translated it?

Joseph Smith consistently described the translation as a gift from God rather than a feat of scholarship — and the details of how he said it happened are more surprising than many people expect.

Joseph never claimed to read the ancient script the way a linguist reads a dead language. He had no training in ancient languages and made no such boast. Instead, he said he translated "by the gift and power of God," using sacred instruments. Early accounts describe two main tools. One was a set of clear stones set in a frame, which the book calls "interpreters" and which came to be associated with the biblical term "Urim and Thummim." The other, described by many eyewitnesses to the process, was a "seer stone" — a smooth stone Joseph had found years earlier.

The most frequently described method is a striking image. According to multiple scribes and observers, Joseph would place the seer stone in the bottom of a hat, put his face into the hat to block out the light, and read aloud the words that appeared to him, while a scribe wrote them down. The golden plates themselves, in these accounts, often were not even open in front of him during the process; they lay covered nearby. The translation, in other words, was described as a revelatory experience, not a study of the physical text.

The church today speaks openly about the seer stone and the hat, and even published photographs of the stone it believes Joseph used. For some, these details feel jarring against the more familiar image of a young man poring over ancient plates. For believers, the method is simply consistent with the claim all along: that the book came by revelation, and that the particular instruments were aids to a fundamentally spiritual gift. As with so much about the Book of Mormon, the documented facts are agreed upon; it is their meaning — miracle or invention — that divides believers and skeptics.

### 28. Who were the "witnesses" to the golden plates?

Because the plates were said to have been returned to the angel and were never available for public examination, the testimony of the witnesses carries real weight in the Latter-day Saint case — and it is one of the more remarkable features of the whole story.

Two groups signed formal statements, printed in nearly every copy of the Book of Mormon since 1830. The Three Witnesses — Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, and Martin Harris — declared that an angel showed them the plates and that they heard the voice of God affirm the translation was correct. Theirs was a visionary, supernatural experience. The Eight Witnesses — several members of the Whitmer and Smith families — gave a more matter-of-fact account: they said they saw the plates plainly, handled them, and "hefted" their weight, describing the engravings and the metal without reference to an angel.

What gives their testimony its force, for believers, is what happened afterward. All three of the Three Witnesses later left the church, and several fell into bitter conflict with Joseph Smith. Yet not one of the eleven is known to have recanted his written statement about the plates. David Whitmer, decades after his break with the church, publicly reaffirmed his testimony rather than retract it. To Latter-day Saints, men had every motive to expose a fraud they had helped create, and none did.

Critics read the same evidence differently. They note that the witnesses were closely tied to Joseph by family and belief, that some described their experience as seeing the plates with "spiritual eyes" rather than ordinary sight, and that a group predisposed to belief can share a sincere but subjective vision. They also point out that not recanting is not the same as producing the plates for the world. As with the rest of the book's origins, the witnesses offer something genuinely difficult to dismiss and genuinely impossible to verify — which is roughly where the honest observer is left.

### 29. What are the Doctrine and Covenants and the Pearl of Great Price?

Alongside the Bible and the Book of Mormon, Latter-day Saints accept two more books as scripture, completing what they call their "standard works" — the four-book canon that defines their doctrine. Each has a distinct character.

The Doctrine and Covenants is the most modern of the four and, in some ways, the most distinctive. Rather than an ancient history, it is a collection of revelations, most given through Joseph Smith in the 1830s and 1840s, addressing the young church directly: how to organize itself, how to build communities, how to understand God's plan, and how to respond to specific crises of the moment. It reads less like narrative and more like a series of divine letters to a growing movement. Uniquely, it remains open — later church presidents have added a small number of items, including the 1978 revelation extending the priesthood to all worthy men regardless of race, and the 1890 declaration ending plural marriage. This is where the Latter-day Saint belief in continuing revelation is most visible on the page.

The Pearl of Great Price is a slim anthology of several shorter texts. It includes the Book of Moses and the Book of Abraham — writings Joseph Smith produced that expand on Genesis and the life of Abraham — along with an inspired retranslation of a portion of Matthew, an excerpt from Joseph Smith's own history recounting the founding events of the faith, and the church's thirteen Articles of Faith, a concise summary of core beliefs.

The Book of Abraham deserves a note, because it too sits at a point of tension. Joseph Smith said he produced it from Egyptian papyri the church acquired in the 1830s. Fragments of those papyri, rediscovered in the 20th century, were dated by Egyptologists to long after Abraham's era and translated as ordinary funerary texts, not the story Joseph gave. Believers offer various explanations — that the papyri were a catalyst for revelation rather than a literal source. Critics see a clear problem. Together, these four books — Bible, Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great Price — form the scriptural world a Latter-day Saint lives within.

### 30. Why do Mormons give the Book of Mormon away for free?

If you have ever been handed a free copy of the Book of Mormon by two young missionaries, you have encountered one of the most visible practices of the faith — and there is a clear logic behind the generosity.

The simplest reason is that Latter-day Saints believe the book is true and that reading it can change a life. Since they regard it as the "keystone" of their religion and a second witness of Jesus Christ, sharing it is, in their view, sharing the most valuable thing they have. Charging money would sit strangely against that conviction; the point is to get the book into hands, not to make a sale. The church funds printing and distribution precisely so cost is never a barrier.

The practice ties directly to Moroni's promise at the end of the book, which invites readers to ask God whether it is true and promises a spiritual answer to the sincere seeker. Missionaries are not really asking you to take their word for it — they are asking you to read the book and pray about it yourself, expecting that the book will do its own persuading. The free copy is an invitation to that personal test, which is why missionaries so often mark a chapter and ask you to read just a few pages before you decide anything.

There is a practical and communal dimension too. Missionary work is central to Latter-day Saint life; tens of thousands of young members serve voluntary missions, and the free book is their primary tool. Handing it out costs the giver nothing but effort, and every copy is a potential conversation. Whether or not you share the faith's convictions, the gesture is sincere on its own terms: they give the book away because they believe it is the best gift they have, and because they trust you to test it for yourself. You are free to read it, set it aside, or simply keep it — the offer comes without a price precisely so the choice can be entirely yours.
