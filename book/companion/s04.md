## Section 4 — History & Origins

### 31. Who was Joseph Smith?

Joseph Smith (1805–1844) was the founder of the movement that became The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. To members he is a prophet, seer, and revelator — the man God chose to restore the original church of Jesus Christ. To critics he is something else entirely. Understanding both readings begins with understanding the boy himself.

He was born into a poor farming family in Vermont and grew up in western New York, in a region so swept by religious revivals that historians call it the "burned-over district." Competing preachers filled the air with rival claims about salvation, and a searching young Smith found the noise bewildering. He had little formal schooling, worked the land with his father and brothers, and by his own account felt caught between the churches, unsure which one to trust.

What happened next is the pivot of the whole story. Smith said that as a teenager he prayed to know which church to join and received a vision (see the next question). Over the following decade he reported further heavenly visitations, most famously an angel named Moroni who directed him to buried golden plates. From those plates, he said, he translated the Book of Mormon, published in 1830. That same year he organized the church.

For the next fourteen years Smith led a fast-growing, often-persecuted community across New York, Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois. He dictated scripture, designed cities, ran for president of the United States, and — privately and controversially — introduced plural marriage. He was killed by a mob in 1844 at age thirty-eight.

However you weigh his claims, Smith's impact is undeniable. A largely unschooled farm boy produced a 500-page scripture, founded a religion now numbering in the millions, and inspired a migration that helped settle the American West. Believers see the hand of God; skeptics see a gifted, complicated man. Nearly two centuries on, that basic disagreement is still where most conversations about him land.

### 32. What was the "First Vision"?

The First Vision is the founding event of the Latter-day Saint faith — the moment members believe God reopened direct communication with humanity after centuries of silence. Around 1820, the teenage Joseph Smith said he went into the woods near his family's farm to pray about which church he should join.

In the account most familiar to members today, written in 1838, Smith described a pillar of light descending until he saw "two Personages" standing above him in the air: God the Father and Jesus Christ. When he asked which church was true, he said he was told to join none of them — that the churches of the day had strayed, and that the true church of Christ would be restored through him. For believers, this single experience establishes the faith's core claims at once: that God and Jesus are separate, embodied beings, and that Smith was called as a prophet.

Here the historical record grows complicated, and honest accounts acknowledge it. Smith left several versions of the story, recorded between 1832 and 1842. They differ in details — how old he was, how many divine beings appeared (an early version mentions the Lord; later ones, two personages), and what exactly was said. Critics argue that the story evolved over time, growing more elaborate and more theologically loaded, which they take as a sign it was embellished or invented.

Believers respond that the accounts were written for different audiences and purposes, that any person retelling a profound experience emphasizes different things at different times, and that the essentials remain consistent: a real divine encounter, a boy told to wait, a restoration to come. The church itself now publishes the varying accounts openly and discusses them in its materials.

You do not have to resolve the dispute to see why it matters. If the First Vision happened as Smith described, the Latter-day Saint claim to restored authority follows. If it did not, the foundation looks very different. For members, faith in the vision is less a conclusion from evidence than a spiritual conviction — something they say they have felt rather than proven.

### 33. How did the Mormon church actually start?

The church was formally organized on April 6, 1830, in upstate New York, but the story runs back several years earlier to the golden plates and the Book of Mormon. According to Joseph Smith, the angel Moroni told him of an ancient record inscribed on gold-colored plates and buried in a nearby hill. In 1827 Smith said he received the plates, and over the next few years he dictated a translation to scribes.

The result, the Book of Mormon, was published in early 1830. It presents itself as the sacred history of ancient peoples in the Americas and, its title page says, as another testament of Jesus Christ. Smith said he translated it "by the gift and power of God," sometimes using seer stones. Believers regard the book as scripture on par with the Bible; critics regard it as Smith's own composition. Either way, the book became the rallying text of the new movement, and early converts often pointed to reading it as the moment they were persuaded.

With scripture in hand, Smith and a small group of followers organized the church. The founding meeting had only a handful of members, but growth was swift. Missionaries fanned out; congregations sprang up in Ohio, then Missouri and Illinois. Converts sold their farms and gathered together, believing they were building the kingdom of God on earth in the last days — which is where the name "Latter-day Saints" comes from.

The early church was not just a set of beliefs but a project of city-building and communal life. Smith laid out plans for temples and gathering places, issued a steady stream of revelations later collected as scripture, and organized a lay priesthood open to worthy male members.

That combination — a charismatic prophet, a new book of scripture, rapid growth, and a tight-knit people who gathered in blocs — was exactly what alarmed their neighbors. Almost from the beginning, the young church's story is also a story of conflict, flight, and persecution.

### 34. Why were the early Mormons persecuted and driven out?

The early Saints were repeatedly driven from their homes — out of New York, Ohio, Missouri, and finally Illinois — and the reasons were as much social and political as religious. Understanding the hostility means seeing how threatening this new community looked to the settlers around it.

First, there was the sheer strangeness of the claims. A young prophet, new scripture, angels, and modern revelation struck many nineteenth-century Protestants as blasphemous. But theology alone rarely drives mobs. What sharpened the fear was that the Saints gathered — they moved into an area by the hundreds and then thousands, bought up land, and built their own tight communities. Existing residents watched newcomers threaten to outnumber them within a few years.

That demographic weight translated into political and economic power. Latter-day Saints tended to vote as a bloc, following their leaders' counsel, which meant they could swing local elections overnight. In frontier Missouri, where the balance between settlers was already tense, this looked like a takeover. Add economic competition, differing views on slavery, and rumors — increasingly, rumors of secret plural marriage — and the mix turned combustible.

Violence followed the Saints from place to place. In Missouri it escalated into open conflict in 1838, with armed clashes on both sides. The state's governor issued an order declaring that the Mormons must be "exterminated or driven from the state" (see question 39). Days later, a militia attacked a small settlement at Haun's Mill, killing seventeen people, including children. The Saints fled Missouri in the winter and regrouped in Illinois.

There they built Nauvoo, for a time one of the largest cities in the state, with its own militia and charter. But the same pattern repeated: growth, political clout, secret polygamy, and mounting suspicion from neighbors, culminating in Joseph Smith's murder in 1844. It would take a thousand-mile migration to the Rocky Mountains before the Saints finally found a place where they, not their neighbors, held the majority.

### 35. What was the Mormon "trek west," and why did they go?

The "trek west" was the great migration, beginning in 1846, in which tens of thousands of Latter-day Saints left the United States and crossed more than a thousand miles of plains and mountains to the Salt Lake Valley in what is now Utah. It is the epic at the emotional center of Latter-day Saint identity, remembered each July with parades and pioneer-day celebrations.

They went because they had run out of places to stay. After Joseph Smith's murder in 1844, hostility toward the Saints in Illinois only grew. Their prosperous city of Nauvoo came under pressure from mobs and the loss of its legal charter, and it became clear the community could not remain. Their new leader, Brigham Young, resolved to take them beyond the reach of the country that had persecuted them — to a place so remote and forbidding that, he hoped, no one would follow.

The journey was brutal. Families packed their lives into covered wagons and set out across Iowa, wintering in makeshift settlements before pushing on along the trail. The first companies reached the Salt Lake Valley in July 1847, where Young, gazing over the arid basin, is said to have declared, "This is the right place." Many thousands followed over the next two decades.

Not all could afford wagons and oxen. Some of the poorest immigrants, many of them European converts, crossed on foot pulling their belongings in handcarts. Most handcart companies arrived safely, but two — the Willie and Martin companies of 1856 — started too late in the season and were caught by early winter storms in Wyoming. More than two hundred people died of cold, exhaustion, and starvation before rescue parties from Salt Lake reached them. The tragedy became one of the most sacred and sorrowful memories in Latter-day Saint history.

For members today, the trek is more than history. It is a story of faith under suffering, retold to children and reenacted by youth groups who pull handcarts across the plains, keeping the memory of the pioneers alive.

### 36. Who was Brigham Young?

Brigham Young (1801–1877) was the man who led the Latter-day Saints west and built their society in the Rocky Mountains — the successor to Joseph Smith and, by most measures, the great organizer of the faith. If Smith was the visionary founder, Young was the builder who turned a scattered, hunted people into a settled commonwealth. Admirers have called him "the American Moses."

Young was a New England carpenter and painter who converted to the church in 1832 after reading the Book of Mormon. He rose quickly through its leadership, and when Smith was murdered in 1844, Young — then head of the church's Quorum of the Twelve Apostles — emerged as the leading candidate to take charge. A majority of the Saints followed him, and within two years he was directing their exodus across the plains.

In the Salt Lake Valley he proved a formidable colonizer. Over three decades he founded hundreds of settlements across the Intermountain West, from Idaho to Arizona, organizing irrigation, agriculture, and industry in a harsh desert. He served as the first governor of the Utah Territory and, in the eyes of his people, as prophet and president of the church. Under his leadership the Saints laid the foundations of Salt Lake City and began building the temple that still anchors it.

Young was also a polarizing figure, and a fair account says so. He practiced plural marriage extensively, with dozens of wives, and defended the doctrine publicly at a time when the church did too. He clashed with the federal government, sometimes to the point of armed standoff. And it was under his administration that the church adopted a restriction barring men of Black African descent from the priesthood — a policy the church did not end until 1978 and has since disavowed the reasoning behind.

Young died in 1877, leaving behind a thriving religious society. Brigham Young University, one of the largest religious universities in the country, bears his name.

### 37. Did the Mormons really practice polygamy?

Yes. For roughly the second half of the nineteenth century, the Latter-day Saints openly practiced plural marriage — one man married to more than one wife — as a religious principle. It is one of the most misunderstood chapters of their history, and it is worth stating plainly at the outset that the mainstream church abandoned the practice more than a century ago and now strictly forbids it (see the next two questions).

Polygamy began quietly. Joseph Smith introduced plural marriage privately in Nauvoo in the early 1840s, teaching a small circle of followers that God had restored the practice of ancient patriarchs like Abraham. He married additional wives in secret, and the rumors that leaked out contributed to the hostility that led to his death. Because it was hidden, much about this early period remains painful and debated, including Smith's marriages to very young women and to women already married to other men — facts the church now acknowledges in its published essays.

Under Brigham Young the practice came into the open. After the move to Utah, the church announced plural marriage publicly in 1852 and defended it as a commandment. Even so, it was never universal: most Latter-day Saint families remained monogamous, and estimates suggest a minority of households — perhaps a quarter to a third at its peak — actually practiced it. Those who did often did so out of religious conviction, believing it brought blessings in this life and the next.

For believers, plural marriage was a divinely commanded trial of faith, later withdrawn by the same divine authority that instituted it. For critics, it was evidence of a religion built around the desires of its male leaders. Both readings have been argued vigorously for well over a century.

What is not in dispute is that it happened, that the church was candid enough to defend it at the time, and that it collided head-on with the laws and moral sensibilities of the United States — a collision that would eventually force the practice to end.

### 38. Why did the Mormons stop practicing polygamy?

The short answer is intense, sustained pressure from the United States government — pressure that threatened the church's very survival. Plural marriage did not fade away gradually; it was ended by a specific decision in 1890 after decades of escalating federal force.

Through the 1860s, 1870s, and 1880s, Congress passed a series of increasingly harsh anti-polygamy laws. They criminalized plural marriage, then stripped polygamists of the right to vote and hold office, then went further: dissolving the church as a legal corporation, seizing its property, and even threatening to confiscate its temples. Church leaders were hunted, arrested, and imprisoned; many went into hiding. By the late 1880s the church faced financial ruin and the loss of the sacred buildings at the heart of its worship.

In 1890 the church's president, Wilford Woodruff, issued a document known as the Manifesto. In it he announced his intention to submit to the laws of the land and advised members to contract no new plural marriages. Members sustained the Manifesto in a general vote. For the church, this was not mere capitulation — Woodruff said he acted under divine direction, shown that continuing would cost the Saints their temples and destroy the work.

The transition was not instant. Some plural marriages quietly continued for a few more years, and it took a second, firmer statement in 1904 to make clear that anyone entering plural marriage would be excommunicated. But the Manifesto marked the decisive turn, and it removed the great obstacle to Utah's acceptance into the Union. Statehood followed in 1896.

This is the crucial point for understanding the church today. The mainstream Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has banned polygamy for more than a century and excommunicates anyone who practices it. The polygamist groups sometimes seen in the news are breakaway fundamentalist sects that rejected the Manifesto — not the mainstream church, which regards them as apostates.

### 39. What was the "Extermination Order"?

The "Extermination Order" was a chilling official directive issued in 1838 by Lilburn Boggs, the governor of Missouri, declaring that the state's Mormon population must be "exterminated or driven from the state" for what he called the public peace. Its blunt language — an American governor calling for a religious group to be exterminated — makes it one of the darkest documents in the history of American religious persecution.

It came at the peak of what is sometimes called the 1838 Mormon War in Missouri. Tensions between the growing Latter-day Saint communities and their neighbors had boiled over into raids, burnings, and armed clashes on both sides. Amid inflamed rumors and real violence, Boggs sided decisively against the Saints and ordered the state militia to treat them as enemies to be removed or destroyed.

The consequences were immediate and deadly. Just three days after the order, a militia force attacked a small Latter-day Saint settlement at Haun's Mill. The residents tried to surrender, but the attackers opened fire, killing seventeen people, among them children who had hidden in a blacksmith's shop. It remains the worst single massacre in the church's history, seared into the community's collective memory.

Under the order, some ten thousand Saints were forced to abandon their homes and property and flee Missouri in the winter of 1838–39, crossing into Illinois with little more than they could carry. Joseph Smith and other leaders were arrested and jailed for months before escaping. The expulsion set the stage for the founding of Nauvoo.

There is a striking historical postscript. The Extermination Order was never formally rescinded until 1976, when Missouri's governor issued an official proclamation apologizing for it and revoking it — nearly a century and a half after it was written. For Latter-day Saints, the order stands as a stark reminder of how far religious hatred once went in America, and of what their ancestors endured to keep their faith.

### 40. How did Utah become the Mormon homeland?

Utah became the Latter-day Saint homeland because it was empty enough, remote enough, and unwanted enough that a persecuted people could finally settle without neighbors to drive them out. When Brigham Young led the first companies into the Salt Lake Valley in 1847, the arid Great Basin lay beyond the settled United States — a place few others coveted. That isolation was precisely the point.

The Saints did not just occupy the valley; they transformed it. In a desert that many considered uninhabitable, they diverted mountain streams to irrigate crops, a pioneering feat of cooperative water management. Over the following decades they spread outward, founding hundreds of towns across present-day Utah and into neighboring territories. Salt Lake City became their capital, planned around a central temple whose construction took forty years.

For a time the Saints hoped to govern themselves with little interference, even proposing a vast territory they called Deseret. But the land had become part of the United States after the war with Mexico in 1848, and the federal government insisted on control. The result was decades of friction: disputes over authority, the stationing of federal troops, and above all the long confrontation over polygamy that stood in the way of statehood.

That obstacle fell in 1890, when the church officially ended plural marriage with the Manifesto (see question 38). With the central conflict resolved, the path to full membership in the Union opened, and Utah became the forty-fifth state in 1896. The territory the Saints had built by hand was now securely their home under American law.

Today Utah remains the demographic and spiritual heart of the faith. The church is headquartered in Salt Lake City, where Temple Square draws visitors from around the world, and Latter-day Saints make up a large share of the state's population. What began as a desperate flight to the ends of the known map became, within a single lifetime, a thriving homeland — the enduring center of a global religion.
